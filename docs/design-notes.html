Catalogue/Design notes

One model, drawn three ways

Each mechanism is a single ES module of parts in millimetres. The interactive stage, the isometric card image and the STL export all read that module — there is no second copy of the geometry anywhere in the project.

Why it is built this way

Most printable-mechanism projects run into the same wall: the animation is drawn by hand in one coordinate system, the CAD model is built in another, and the STL comes from a third. Then a parameter changes and the three drift apart — the picture still moves, but the export is wrong, and nobody notices because nobody compares them.

Here the module is the comparison. A part is a list of extruded 2D profiles with a height and a place in the stack. The viewer extrudes them into triangles. The exporter writes those same triangles to STL. The card image culls and sorts those same faces. If the picture looks right and the STL is wrong, that is a bug you can find, because both come from the same numbers.

Parts

A part carries its solids (in its own print-flat frame), the place it sits in the assembled machine (at), the point it pivots about (pivot), and its role. Two kinds exist: print parts, which are exported, and hardware parts, which are drawn but never exported. Repeated parts share a printId, so the three identical caps are one STL with a quantity of three.

Pose

Nothing about the geometry changes when the machine moves — every part is rigid, and the pose only says how far each one has turned or slid. That is the real kinematic constraint of the machines in this catalogue, and it means the stage is fast: the meshes are built once per parameter change, and a frame is a handful of transform updates.

Readouts

Every number on the page is computed from the same functions that place the parts. The slider position is the slider's own position, the ratio is the tooth counts that cut the gears, and the cam's slope and curvature are finite differences of the law that generated the outline.

How a part becomes an STL

  1. Profiles. Gear, rack, cam, ratchet, Geneva and pawl profiles are generated in src/lib/profiles.js; plain plates, discs and links in src/lib/kit.js.
  2. Extrude. Each profile is sliced into horizontal bands at every vertex height. Inside a band every edge is a straight segment, so pairing them left to right (even-odd) gives the solid intervals between outline and holes. Each band boundary is split at every vertex on that line, so neighbouring strips share vertices exactly and side walls can be taken straight from the cap's own boundary edges. No T-junctions, and no dependence on hole-bridging or ear-clipping, both of which failed here first.
  3. Check. Winding, closedness, overlap and area are verified per solid, per part, at every parameter extreme, and before every page build.
  4. Quantise. Coordinates snap to 0.1 µm so the float32 STL is bit-identical along shared edges.
  5. Write. Binary STL, millimetres, one file per printed part, zipped with a sheet and a bill of materials.

What went wrong on the way (and what fixed it)

SymptomCauseFix
Meshes were not closed around holes The triangulator bridged holes into the outline and then stalled on the pinch vertices the bridge creates. Replaced with a scanline decomposition, which has no bridges to stall on. It also removed a 60-second hang.
A part looked fine but the mesh reported missing reverse edges Caps and walls were built from different vertex sets: interpolation along a cap edge produced a vertex the wall ring did not contain. Walls are now derived from the cap's boundary edges, and the scanline splits band lines at every vertex.
The cam triangulated to a smaller area than its outline enclosed The cam outline doubled back on itself at angles the generator rounds off; the triangulator returned a plausible-looking but wrong result. The triangulator now checks that the area it covers equals the area the rings enclose, and throws if not. The cam generator also reports undercut and folds.
The isometric card showed only the base plate Faces were sorted near-to-far instead of far-to-near. Sort direction fixed, and the base frame is now drawn before the parts standing on it, because a flat-shaded painter's sort cannot resolve a part that sits inside another's silhouette.
Gear teeth vanished into the plate Interlocking teeth cannot be depth-sorted correctly with flat faces. The parts that interlock (pinion, pawl) carry an explicit draw priority, so their near teeth are always legible.
The ZIP would not open in some tools The buffer was allocated 22 bytes too long, leaving trailing zeros after the end-of-directory record. A round-trip test reads the pack back with its own ZIP reader and verifies every CRC.

Testing

npm test runs three suites. The mesh suite builds every profile at every parameter range and proves the solids are closed and the right volume. The mechanism suite builds every mechanism at every parameter extreme and asserts printability, real readouts and valid steps. The export suite round-trips the print pack, checks the ZIP, and proves that broken geometry is reported rather than exported.

npm run build regenerates every page, card and the catalogue; it refuses to publish a mechanism whose pack fails. npm run export writes a pack per mechanism from the command line, with any parameters you like:

node src/export-cli.js cam-follower lift=12 rise=90 law=parabolic
node src/export-cli.js --all --out=dist

Credits and sources

The mechanisms are the classics: Henry T. Brown’s Five Hundred and Seven Mechanical Movements (1868), the standard kinematics of the slider-crank, involute gears and cam followers, and the anatomy craft rules for the isometric figures — one object, real parts, true numbers, one lit accent. The follow-on list follows the physical-computing shelf: servos, cranks, cams, racks, ratchets and indexers.
