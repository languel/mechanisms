Catalogue/Print standard

The print standard

Every mechanism in the catalogue is built from the same small vocabulary of dimensions, so a post from one kit fits a cap from another, and anything you learn about one machine applies to the next.

1. The stack

Parts are designed in millimetres, print-flat: z = 0 is the bed face and +z is up. The assembled machine stacks the same parts along +z, one turning layer at a time.

ElementDimensionWhy
Base plate4 mmStiff enough for a 200 mm plate, prints in 20 layers.
Links, gears, cams, levers6 mmThree 0.4 mm perimeters plus infill; strong in the plane.
Turning layer pitch8 mm6 mm body + 1.6 mm hub + 0.4 mm running clearance.
Hub (stand-off boss)1.6 mm tallLifts a part off the layer below without a bought spacer.
Pivot postØ6.8 mm, printedStrong enough in PLA; a Ø3 rod fits the same bore.
Press capØ13.2 mm × 3 mmHolds a link on a post; 0.3 mm interference.

2. Fits

FitNominalUsed for
Running+0.30 mm on the postEvery bore that turns or slides. Adjustable per mechanism: 0.2 mm for a well-tuned printer, 0.5 mm for a loose one.
Press−0.30 mm on the postCaps, and anything meant to stay put. A drop of glue is allowed but usually unnecessary.
Clearance0.35 mm per sideWhere a part passes beside another: carriages between rails, racks through keepers.

Fits are parametric, not fixed. Each page exposes the clearance and re-cuts every bore and every gap from it. If your printer runs tight, raise it and re-export — the STLs and the sheet both follow.

3. What counts as printable

Before a page is published, every printed part is mesh-checked. A part is publishable when:

  • every solid in it is closed: each directed edge is matched by the same number of its reverse, so a slicer sees a closed surface;
  • every solid is wound outwards, proved by a positive signed volume;
  • no two solids in the same part overlap in both height and footprint — stacked bosses may touch a plate, never sink into it, because the slicer's even-odd fill would carve the overlap out of the part;
  • the profile triangulates cleanly: the cap area equals the area the rings enclose, which catches a hole that has drifted across the outline (a real failure that the first version of the cam generator produced);
  • it fits the bed: every part must be smaller than 220 mm in every direction.

The same checks run in three places: in the page (every parameter change), in npm test (at every parameter extreme of every mechanism), and in src/build.js (before the page is written). The exports are float32-quantised to 0.1 µm first, so the vertices a slicer sees are bit-identical across a shared edge — the check that the file is watertight is the same check a slicer runs.

4. Orientation and supports

Every STL is already in its printing orientation: flat face on the bed, tallest feature up. No part needs supports. Bridges are kept under 16 mm (the rack keepers are 12 mm) and overhangs stay below 50° except where they are short enough to print clean.

5. Hardware

Most kits need no bought parts at all: posts and pins are printed and retained by press caps. Where a mechanism needs a real axle or a real servo, the page lists it in Hardware and the sheet counts it. The two standard sizes are Ø3 mm rod (with 0.3 mm running clearance) and Ø2 mm steel pin for the small linkages.

6. Mass and time

Each page reports the printed mass from the model's own volume at PLA density (1.24 g/cm³) and the same figure appears in the pack, per part and in total. Add roughly 25–30 g per hour of printing on a 0.4 mm nozzle at 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters and 25% infill.

Where these numbers came from. The 8 mm layer pitch and the 0.3 mm running fit were chosen by printing: a 6 mm link with a 1.6 mm hub clears the plate by the 0.4 mm left over, and a Ø6.8 printed post in a Ø7.1 bore turns freely with no post-processing. Anything looser rattles; anything tighter needs drilling, which defeats the point of a printable kit.
