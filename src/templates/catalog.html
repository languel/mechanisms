Why these mechanisms first

They are the vocabulary of physical computing

A servo horn becomes a crank, a crank becomes a slider, a slider becomes a rocker. Almost every small machine you would build with a microcontroller — a plotter, a camera slider, a robot leg, a feeder — is two or three of these movements joined together.

The catalogue starts with the movements you can hold in one hand, because those are the ones you need to feel before a linkage diagram means anything.

Each page is a machine, not an article

One mechanism per page: the object, the controls, the cutaway, the numbers, and one sentence that says what the whole thing is about. No diagram is decorative — every part you see is a part in the STL beside it.

The breakdown is written from the model: the stroke really is twice the crank radius, the rod ratio really does set the side load, the cam really does undercut when you push the roller past the flank.

Print packs, not just STLs

Every page exports a ZIP: one STL per part, already flat on the bed and in millimetres; a parameter sheet with the values you chose; a bill of materials with masses; and the assembly order.

Repeated parts are printed once and listed with their quantity, so three identical caps are one file, not three.

The print standard

Every kit in the catalogue shares one set of dimensions, so parts from different mechanisms fit each other:

  • Base plate 4 mm; links, gears, cams and levers 6 mm.
  • One turning layer every 8 mm, each on its own 1.6 mm hub.
  • Pivots are printed posts; parts are held on by press-fit caps.
  • 0.3 mm running clearance on every bore, 0.3 mm interference on every press fit.
  • Minimum 2.4 mm wall around a bore, 1.6 mm web elsewhere.

Nothing in a pack needs supports, and nothing needs a screw. A kit is a plate, a few links, a few caps and a printed post — plus, on some mechanisms, one bought rod.

Every STL is checked before it is written: each solid must be closed, wound outwards, and free of overlaps with the other solids in the same part. The page will not publish a mechanism that fails.

What comes next

Next in the queue

  • Four-bar linkage — change the direction and length of a push
  • Bell crank — turn a pull through 90° without a cable
  • Spring return — the other half of every solenoid-driven mechanism
  • Scotch yoke — a dwell for free, at the cost of side load
  • Worm drive — a printed gearbox that will not back-drive

After that

  • Barrel cam and grooved cam — motion the roller cannot leave
  • Planetary stage — a printed gearbox for a hobby motor
  • Toggle clamp — the mechanism that locks itself
  • Bevel pair — gearing that turns a corner
  • Linkage explorer: drag any joint and read the coupler curve

How it is built

Each mechanism is a small ES module: parameters, a build() that returns parts in print-flat millimetres, a pose() that moves them, and the readouts. The page, the card image and the STL export all read from that one module — no second copy of the geometry anywhere.
